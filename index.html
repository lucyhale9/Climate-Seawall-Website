How High Should The Seawall Be?

A coastal city is about to build a seawall it expects to last until 2100. Today's storms are only part of the risk. Sea level keeps rising, so every storm in the future starts from higher water. Move the sliders to see how often the wall would be overtopped over its lifetime.

The Issue

Picture the ocean on a calm day. When a big storm comes through, wind and low air pressure push the water above that calm level for a few hours. That temporary bump is the storm surge. A seawall only has to be taller than the calm sea plus the surge.

Here's the catch: sea level rise raises the starting line. Even if storms never get any stronger, the same storm in 2080 rides on top of higher water than it does today, so it climbs higher up the wall. A wall that would only be overtopped by a once-in-500-years storm today might be overtopped by a once-in-20-years storm by the end of the century. The city has to choose a height now for water levels that will keep changing for the next 75 years.

What To Notice Moving The Sliders

  • The curve bends upward. Even with storms that never change, the chance of overtopping grows every year because the starting water level keeps rising. Compare the blue line with the gray dashed "no sea level rise" line. The gap between them is the risk sea level rise adds.
  • The sliders work together. Set the wall to 2.5 m. With 0.3 m of sea level rise the wall stays mostly safe until 2100, but with 1.5 m it falls below "100-year" protection in the 2060s and is overtopped roughly once every 8 years by the 2090s. The same wall can be a good choice in one future and a bad choice in another.
  • Small changes in height matter a lot. Each extra ~25 cm of wall cuts the yearly chance by about a factor of 2.7, and each extra ~25 cm of sea level rise multiplies it by the same amount. That's why a few tenths of a meter can change the big number dramatically.
  • "1% per year" isn't "safe for 100 years." Even a wall that stays exactly at 1% per year has about a 53% chance of being overtopped at least once over 75 years. Watch the big number. It counts every year of the wall's life.
  • Stormier coastlines need taller walls. Raise "today's 100-year storm surge" and the whole curve jumps up. Sea level rise then pushes it up further on top of that.

How The Math Works

1. Storm surge. We treat the biggest storm surge each year as a random draw from a Gumbel distribution, the standard tool for "largest event of the year." Small surges are common and big ones are rare. The chance that one year's biggest surge exceeds a height x is:

P(surge > x) = 1 − exp( −exp( −(x − μ) / β ) )

β (the "scale") controls how quickly big surges get rarer. We set μ so that the surge with a 1% yearly chance equals your "today's 100-year storm surge" slider value.

2. Sea level rise. Average sea level starts at today's level and rises to your 2100 value along a smooth curve that starts at today's observed rate and speeds up over time, like the IPCC and NOAA projections.

3. Overtopping in one year. The wall is overtopped if sea level that year + that year's biggest surge > wall height. So the surge only has to exceed wall height − sea level rise so far, and we plug that into the formula above.

4. Lifetime chance. Assuming each year is independent, the chance the wall is never overtopped is (1 − pyear 1) × (1 − pyear 2) × … × (1 − p2100). The big number is 1 minus that.

Simplifications we made (worth discussing)
  • Storms are assumed to stay the same strength and frequency. Only the baseline moves. Real hurricanes may get stronger with warming, which would make things worse.
  • "Overtopping" means still water above the top of the wall. Waves can splash over a wall even when the still water is lower, and tides add to or subtract from surge. We fold all of this into one "storm water level."
  • Local land sinking (subsidence) adds to sea level rise in many cities and isn't modeled separately. You could fold it into the sea level rise slider.
  • The wall doesn't settle, erode, or get raised later. Real cities often plan to raise walls in stages.

Where The Numbers Come From

Values marked placeholder were chosen to be physically reasonable but still need to be checked against, or replaced with, numbers from our ATMO 215 course readings.

  • Sea level rise by 2100: slider range 0.3–2.0 m, default 0.75 m placeholder
    The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6, Working Group I, 2021) gives likely global-mean rise by 2100 of roughly 0.3–0.6 m for low emissions and 0.6–1.0 m for very high emissions. It also says rise approaching 2 m can't be ruled out if the ice sheets become unstable. The U.S. NOAA 2022 Sea Level Rise Technical Report uses scenarios from 0.3 m (Low) to 2.0 m (High) by 2100.
  • Today's rate of sea level rise: 4 mm per year (where the curve starts) placeholder
    Satellite measurements (NASA/NOAA altimetry) show global sea level rising about 3–4.5 mm per year in recent years, and the rate is speeding up.
  • Today's 100-year storm surge: slider range 1.0–4.0 m, default 2.0 m placeholder
    Order of magnitude for extreme storm water levels above average sea level at U.S. tide gauges. It's about 1–2 m on many sheltered coasts and several meters on hurricane-exposed Gulf and Atlantic coasts. Should be replaced with a specific city's tide-gauge value.
  • Gumbel scale β = 0.25 m (how fast big surges get rarer) placeholder
    Typical values from extreme-value fits to U.S. tide gauges are roughly 0.1–0.3 m. This number decides how strongly sea level rise multiplies the overtopping risk: every β meters of rise multiplies the yearly chance by about e ≈ 2.7. This is the reason studies find that today's "100-year" floods could happen every year or so by 2100 at many locations (IPCC Special Report on the Ocean and Cryosphere, 2019).
  • Seawall height: slider range 1.0–5.0 m, default 2.5 m placeholder
    Covers a range from low flood walls to large urban storm barriers.
